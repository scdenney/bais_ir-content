<section class="content-section prose" aria-labelledby="why-inquiry">
  <h2 id="why-inquiry">Why use classroom inquiry?</h2>
  <p>International relations makes claims about how people perceive risk and define their interests or identities. A classroom study makes some of those claims easier to inspect. Students see how a hypothesis becomes a treatment and how an abstract concept becomes a measure. They also learn why evidence may be inconclusive or run against the hypothesis.</p>

  <p>The studies serve a pedagogical purpose. They are classroom activities with a voluntary convenience group from one course rather than preregistered population studies. Random assignment can make the comparison informative within that group. It cannot make the class representative of a wider population.</p>

  <h2 id="design-types">Three kinds of evidence</h2>
  <div class="lecture-map">
    <div><strong>Experiment</strong><p>Participants are assigned to different materials before answering the same outcome questions. The comparison concerns the materials as implemented and remains subject to limits in sample size or implementation.</p></div>
    <div><strong>Survey</strong><p>All participants receive the same questions. The results describe the participating group. They identify no causal effect and provide no population estimate.</p></div>
    <div><strong>Exercise</strong><p>A show of hands or discussion prompt structures collective reasoning. It produces no result dataset unless responses are recorded.</p></div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="content-section prose" id="week-2" aria-labelledby="week-2-title">
  <p class="eyebrow">Week 2 · Randomized survey experiment</p>
  <h2 id="week-2-title">Framing U.S.–China cooperation</h2>
  <div class="study-record">
    <div><h3>Question</h3><p>Does a Hobbesian rivalry brief or a Lockean cooperation brief change expectations about whether the United States and China can cooperate?</p></div>
    <div><h3>Conditions</h3><p>Participants received either a rivalry-oriented analyst brief or a cooperation-oriented one. The implemented control received no analyst brief. This corrects the earlier site's inaccurate description of the control as a neutral “snapshot” vignette.</p></div>
    <div><h3>Outcome</h3><p>Five 1–5 items formed a Cooperation Perception Score after two items were reverse-coded. Higher scores represented more optimism about cooperation.</p></div>
    <div><h3>What it teaches</h3><p>The activity shows how theoretical language becomes a treatment and why a multi-item scale requires coding decisions. A predicted ordering does not itself constitute a reliable effect.</p></div>
  </div>
  <p class="method-note">The briefs contain different claims and examples, so the design tests each narrative as a whole. Any summary of the study should state that the substantive content changed along with the wording.</p>
</section>

<section class="content-section prose" id="week-3" aria-labelledby="week-3-title">
  <p class="eyebrow">Week 3 · Randomized survey experiment</p>
  <h2 id="week-3-title">Framing nuclear safety</h2>
  <div class="study-record">
    <div><h3>Question</h3><p>Does emphasizing deterrence and stability, danger and near-misses, or a descriptive overview change assessments of whether nuclear weapons stabilize international politics?</p></div>
    <div><h3>Conditions</h3><p>The Stability vignette centered on deterrence and the long peace. The Danger vignette discussed accidents and false alarms as well as the erosion of arms control. A third condition received a descriptive Control vignette.</p></div>
    <div><h3>Outcome</h3><p>Five 1–5 items formed a Nuclear Safety Perception Score after two items were reverse-coded. Higher scores represented a more stabilizing assessment.</p></div>
    <div><h3>What it teaches</h3><p>The activity translates the Waltz–Sagan debate into competing expectations. It also requires students to distinguish arguments supplied in the treatment from claims supported by the comparison.</p></div>
  </div>
  <p class="method-note">The vignettes supply arguments for participants to assess. The survey does not establish those arguments as facts, and this site makes no causal claim about the realized student comparison.</p>
</section>

<section class="content-section prose" id="week-4" aria-labelledby="week-4-title">
  <p class="eyebrow">Week 4 · Randomized survey experiment</p>
  <h2 id="week-4-title">Historical analogies and strategic advice</h2>
  <div class="study-record">
    <div><h3>Question</h3><p>Does a European balance-of-power analogy or an Asian institutional-engagement analogy change recommendations for smaller states facing a fictional rising power?</p></div>
    <div><h3>Conditions</h3><p>Participants received a European historical brief, an Asian historical brief or a control scenario without either analogy.</p></div>
    <div><h3>Outcome</h3><p>Five 1–5 items formed an Engagement Orientation Score after two items were reverse-coded. Higher scores represented more engagement-oriented advice.</p></div>
    <div><h3>What it teaches</h3><p>The activity examines how historical experience enters strategic reasoning. It also asks whether the instrument reproduces the regional simplifications that scholars of Global IR question.</p></div>
  </div>
  <p class="method-note">Historical analogy is one of several differences between the vignettes. Their substantive content also changes, and the control contains additional facts. The exercise therefore compares complete briefs.</p>
</section>

<section class="content-section prose" id="week-10" aria-labelledby="week-10-title">
  <p class="eyebrow">Week 10 · Descriptive classroom survey</p>
  <h2 id="week-10-title">Criteria of national membership</h2>
  <div class="study-record">
    <div><h3>Question</h3><p>Which criteria do participants consider important for being “truly” part of a nation? How do those criteria relate to ethnic, civic and voluntarist conceptions of membership?</p></div>
    <div><h3>Instrument</h3><p>The survey measured citizen preference and included an eight-item membership battery. It covered birth and ancestry, citizenship and residence, language, religion, respect for institutions and felt membership. Background questions and optional feedback completed the instrument.</p></div>
    <div><h3>Comparison</h3><p>The analysis workflow compared selected items with weighted ISSP 2023 benchmarks for four countries. These were descriptive comparisons, and some classroom items lacked a directly comparable benchmark.</p></div>
    <div><h3>What it teaches</h3><p>The activity translates abstract definitions of nationalism into measurement choices. It also distinguishes a descriptive classroom pattern from a representative country estimate.</p></div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="content-section prose" id="week-11" aria-labelledby="week-11-title">
  <p class="eyebrow">Week 11 · Structured exercise</p>
  <h2 id="week-11-title">Diagnosing the globalization trilemma</h2>
  <div class="study-record">
    <div><h3>Question</h3><p>Which corner of Rodrik's trilemma was constrained in five political-economic cases? The possible answers were deep globalization, national sovereignty and democratic politics.</p></div>
    <div><h3>Cases</h3><p>Greece, Brexit, China, the United States under Trump, and Argentina.</p></div>
    <div><h3>Format</h3><p>Students voted by show of hands before the lecture worked through each diagnosis.</p></div>
    <div><h3>Evidence status</h3><p>No vote tally or participant count was recorded. Expected splits in the speaker notes guided the discussion and should not be treated as classroom findings.</p></div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="content-section prose" id="week-12" aria-labelledby="week-12-title">
  <p class="eyebrow">Week 12 · Descriptive survey and course review</p>
  <h2 id="week-12-title">The Anthropocene and global futures</h2>
  <div class="study-record">
    <div><h3>Question</h3><p>How do participants understand the Anthropocene and assess whether IR and sovereignty are adequate to it? The survey also asked about ecological conflict, future priorities and the course itself.</p></div>
    <div><h3>Instrument</h3><p>The instrument included a concept check and five agreement items. It also contained one future-priority choice, five usefulness ratings and an optional open-text question.</p></div>
    <div><h3>Implementation note</h3><p>An earlier draft proposed a ranking of five future forces. The fielded instrument used a single-choice priority item.</p></div>
    <div><h3>What it teaches</h3><p>Formative assessment and course review can share an instrument even though neither component constitutes an experiment.</p></div>
  </div>
  <p class="method-note">Open-text feedback remains private. The fielded activity was a non-randomized survey. An earlier placeholder page incorrectly described a proposed framing experiment as though it had been run.</p>
</section>

<section class="content-section prose" aria-labelledby="interpretation-rules">
  <h2 id="interpretation-rules">Interpretation rules</h2>
  <ol class="takeaway-list">
    <li><div>Call a study an experiment only when an implemented assignment mechanism created comparison conditions.</div></li>
    <li><div>Describe the treatment that was actually fielded, including a no-brief control or bundled differences between vignettes.</div></li>
    <li><div>Separate hypotheses and claims made in the treatment from findings. A null or reversed result can still serve the pedagogical purpose.</div></li>
    <li><div>Do not generalize a voluntary classroom group to a population.</div></li>
    <li><div>Keep student-level responses, identifying fields, and open-text feedback out of the public repository.</div></li>
  </ol>
</section>
