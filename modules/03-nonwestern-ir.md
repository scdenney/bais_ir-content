## Overview {#overview}

This lecture turns from using international relations theories to examining where those theories came from and which historical experiences they privilege. The discipline developed largely through Euro-American institutions and cases. Its standard concepts—anarchy, sovereignty, balance of power, and the state—therefore raise a basic question: do they describe universal political processes, or particular histories elevated into general theory?

Asian historical orders, the Chinese tributary system, Southeast Asian mandala politics, and non-Western intellectual traditions supply alternative starting points. Amitav Acharya's work organizes three possible responses: extend established theories, defend regional exceptionalism, or build Global IR by deprovincializing the discipline and tracing how ideas are localized. The lecture does not propose replacing one canon with another. It asks how theory improves when scholars broaden its historical evidence while avoiding new regional parochialisms.

### Questions guiding the lecture

<ol class="question-list">
  <li><div>In what sense has IR theory been shaped by Western institutions and historical experience?</div></li>
  <li><div>Can a theory be universal when the cases from which it was built are regionally narrow?</div></li>
  <li><div>What distinguishes non-Western theory-building from simply applying existing theory to a different region?</div></li>
  <li><div>How do hierarchy, engagement, and institutional binding complicate balance-of-power accounts of Asian order?</div></li>
</ol>

## Classroom study {#classroom-study}

<div class="study-panel">
  <p class="eyebrow">Randomized survey experiment</p>
  <h3>Historical analogies and strategic advice</h3>
  <p><strong>Question.</strong> Does presenting a fictional regional-security problem through a European balance-of-power analogy or an Asian institutional-engagement analogy change recommendations for smaller states?</p>
  <p><strong>Design.</strong> Qualtrics evenly presented European, Asian, or control briefs. Five 1–5 items formed an Engagement Orientation Score after two items were reverse-coded.</p>
  <p><strong>Interpretive limit.</strong> The vignettes bundle historical analogies with different substantive claims, and the control contains information not present in both treatments. The exercise therefore compares complete briefs; it does not isolate “European” versus “Asian” history as a single treatment ingredient.</p>
</div>

<p class="method-note">The public record describes the implemented design without presenting student responses or asserting that the exercise confirmed Acharya's argument. Its pedagogical value lies partly in asking whether the instrument itself reproduces the simplifications it is meant to examine.</p>

## Takeaways {#takeaways}

<ol class="takeaway-list">
  <li><div>Theories reflect the histories and institutions from which they are built; provenance is part of evaluating their scope.</div></li>
  <li><div>Hierarchy and relational authority complicate accounts that assume sovereign equality and anarchy as universal starting points.</div></li>
  <li><div>Global IR aims to expand and recombine the discipline, not replace one regional canon with another.</div></li>
  <li><div>Asian cases can generate concepts and mechanisms, not merely test theories developed elsewhere.</div></li>
  <li><div>Knowledge production is institutional: security dependence, language, publication incentives, and academic networks shape which theories travel.</div></li>
</ol>
