## Overview {#overview}

This lecture explains why globalization's expansion of connectivity has also generated social dislocation and political backlash. Heywood supplies the sociological layer: durable communities coexist with increasingly individualized and digitally mediated ties, while precarious work, risk, and network power weaken stable sources of belonging.

Dani Rodrik supplies the political-economic constraint. Deep globalization, national sovereignty, and democratic politics cannot all be maintained at full strength, and late-stage liberalization creates concentrated losses that compensation often fails to repair. Pankaj Mishra adds an affective layer by interpreting resentment and rage as responses to modernity's broken promises. The lecture does not reduce populism to a single cause. It distinguishes demand from political supply and compares left-populist, right-populist, and institutionally absorbed forms across regions.

### Questions guiding the lecture

<ol class="question-list">
  <li><div>How do communication technologies and markets transform community and social belonging?</div></li>
  <li><div>Which combinations of globalization, sovereignty, and democracy are politically sustainable?</div></li>
  <li><div>How do economic loss, cultural change, and status anxiety interact without becoming interchangeable explanations?</div></li>
  <li><div>Why does populist demand consolidate into durable political movements in some settings but not others?</div></li>
</ol>

  <h3>Diagnosing the globalization trilemma</h3>
  <p><strong>Question.</strong> Which corner—deep globalization, national sovereignty, or democratic politics—was constrained in Greece, Brexit, China, the United States under Trump, and Argentina?</p>

## Takeaways {#takeaways}

<ol class="takeaway-list">
  <li><div>Global connection does not produce social convergence; it can coexist with precarity, fragmentation, and renewed demands for control.</div></li>
  <li><div>Rodrik's trilemma clarifies institutional trade-offs but does not by itself explain which political coalition forms in response.</div></li>
  <li><div>Economic and cultural accounts of populism can interact. Neither should be treated as a universal, single-cause explanation.</div></li>
  <li><div>Populist outcomes depend on political supply: institutions, leaders, parties, and regional histories shape how discontent is organized.</div></li>
</ol>
