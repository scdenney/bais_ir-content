## Overview {#overview}

Why has greater global connectivity also produced social dislocation and political backlash? Heywood locates part of the answer in social relations. Durable communities coexist with more individualized and digitally mediated ties. Precarious work and network power can also weaken stable sources of belonging.

Dani Rodrik identifies a political-economic constraint. Deep globalization, national sovereignty and democratic politics cannot all be maintained at full strength. Late-stage liberalization also creates concentrated losses that compensation often fails to repair. Pankaj Mishra interprets resentment and rage as responses to modernity's broken promises. Together, the readings support a multicausal explanation of populism. The lecture distinguishes demand from political supply and compares how regions organize discontent differently.

### Questions guiding the lecture

<ol class="question-list">
  <li><div>How do communication technologies and markets transform community and social belonging?</div></li>
  <li><div>Which combinations of globalization, sovereignty, and democracy are politically sustainable?</div></li>
  <li><div>How do economic loss, cultural change, and status anxiety interact without becoming interchangeable explanations?</div></li>
  <li><div>Why does populist demand consolidate into durable political movements in some settings but not others?</div></li>
</ol>

## Lecture map {#lecture-map}

<div class="lecture-map">
  <div><strong>Part 1</strong><p>Social transformation changes both thick and thin forms of connection. Precarity and risk society provide one account of that change, while liquid modernity and network society provide others.</p></div>
  <div><strong>Part 2</strong><p>Rodrik's globalization trilemma introduces the political economy. Trade creates distributional losses, and compensation often fails politically.</p></div>
  <div><strong>Part 3</strong><p>Mishra explains dislocation and humiliation as sources of resentment and rage. This affective account adds one layer to contemporary backlash.</p></div>
  <div><strong>Part 4</strong><p>The analysis separates populist demand from political supply. Institutions can absorb or diffuse discontent, and they can channel it into left- or right-wing movements.</p></div>
</div>

## Classroom study {#classroom-study}

<div class="study-panel">
  <p class="eyebrow">Structured lecture exercise</p>
  <h3>Diagnosing the globalization trilemma</h3>
  <p>The exercise asks which corner of Rodrik's trilemma was constrained in five cases. Students considered Greece and Brexit, followed by China, the United States under Trump and Argentina.</p>
  <p>Students voted by show of hands before the lecture worked through each case. The activity served as a discussion and diagnostic exercise rather than a survey or randomized experiment.</p>

<p class="method-note">A planned answer and a show-of-hands discussion have a different evidentiary status from a stored survey result. This site makes no empirical claim about how the class voted.</p>

## Takeaways {#takeaways}

<ol class="takeaway-list">
  <li><div>Global connection can coexist with precarity and social fragmentation. It may also generate renewed demands for control.</div></li>
  <li><div>Rodrik's trilemma clarifies institutional trade-offs but does not by itself explain which political coalition forms in response.</div></li>
  <li><div>Economic and cultural accounts of populism can interact. Neither should be treated as a universal, single-cause explanation.</div></li>
  <li><div>Populist outcomes depend on political supply. Institutions and regional histories shape how leaders and parties organize discontent.</div></li>
</ol>
