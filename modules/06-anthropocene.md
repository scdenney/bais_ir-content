## Overview {#overview}

The final lecture uses the Anthropocene to test whether international relations has an adequate toolkit for planetary-scale problems. Audra Mitchell's provocation comes first: a discipline organized around survival often treats extinction as large-scale death or managerial risk, missing the loss of life's open-ended capacity for being and becoming. Her posthumanist response decenters humanity and takes nonhuman and inhuman forces seriously.

The lecture then asks how images of global futures do political work across artificial intelligence, outer space, ecology and planetary boundaries, and migration. Who writes the rules? Who gains from technological acceleration? Who bears adaptation costs? After the break, these questions reconnect the course's major themes: constructivism and Global IR, nationalism and populism, and the globalization trilemma. The closing lesson is methodological. IR theories are tools with histories and limits; students should keep asking what each framework makes visible and what it leaves out.

### Questions guiding the lecture

<ol class="question-list">
  <li><div>Why does extinction pose an ontological challenge rather than only another empirical security risk?</div></li>
  <li><div>How do AI, space, ecological change, and migration distribute agency and vulnerability across human and nonhuman life?</div></li>
  <li><div>Whose futures are prioritized, secured, delayed, or sacrificed in present political decisions?</div></li>
  <li><div>How do the theories studied across the course enable—and constrain—the imagination of alternatives?</div></li>
</ol>

## Classroom study {#classroom-study}

  <h3>Concepts, future priorities, and course reflection</h3>
  <p><strong>Question.</strong> Do participants recognize the Anthropocene concept, how do they assess IR, sovereignty, technology, and ecological conflict, which future challenge do they prioritize, and how useful did they find course components?</p>

## Takeaways {#takeaways}

<ol class="takeaway-list">
  <li><div>Planetary processes do not respect sovereign boundaries, levels of analysis, or the human-centered ontology on which much IR depends.</div></li>
  <li><div>Extinction is more than a large death toll: it concerns the disappearance of unique forms of life and future possibility.</div></li>
  <li><div>Future scenarios are present political interventions. They authorize investment, control, delay, sacrifice, and resistance.</div></li>
  <li><div>Technology, ecology, and migration are not separate “emerging issues”; they are connected sites where power distributes risk and agency.</div></li>
  <li><div>A critical user of theory asks not only whether a model explains, but whose experience and whose future its categories include.</div></li>
</ol>
