## Overview {#overview}

This lecture puts the preceding theoretical toolkit to work on nuclear proliferation. It begins with the security dilemma: measures intended to make one state safer can make others less secure, producing an action–reaction spiral even when neither side seeks war. From there, the lecture distinguishes deterrence from proliferation, horizontal from vertical spread, and a secure second-strike capability from the mere possession of weapons.

Scott Sagan's three models organize the central explanation of acquisition. States may seek nuclear weapons in response to external threats, because domestic organizations and political coalitions favor them, or because the bomb carries status and normative meaning. North Korea shows why these explanations are usually complements rather than substitutes. The lecture then stages the Waltz–Sagan dispute over whether nuclear proliferation stabilizes or endangers international politics and closes with the NPT, IAEA safeguards, and arms-control challenges.

### Questions guiding the lecture

<ol class="question-list">
  <li><div>Why do states build nuclear weapons, and which mechanisms distinguish Sagan's three models?</div></li>
  <li><div>When does deterrence produce stability, and when can uncertainty, accidents, or organizations undermine it?</div></li>
  <li><div>How does the North Korean case combine security, domestic, and normative motives?</div></li>
  <li><div>What can the NPT and arms-control institutions accomplish when states retain strong security incentives?</div></li>
</ol>

## Classroom study {#classroom-study}

<div class="study-panel">
  <p class="eyebrow">Randomized survey experiment</p>
  <h3>Framing nuclear safety</h3>
  <p><strong>Question.</strong> Does a deterrence-and-stability brief, a danger-and-near-miss brief, or a descriptive control change how participants assess the stabilizing effects of nuclear weapons?</p>
  <p><strong>Design.</strong> Qualtrics evenly presented one of three vignettes. Participants answered five 1–5 items combined into a Nuclear Safety Perception Score after two items were reverse-coded.</p>
  <p><strong>Interpretive limit.</strong> Each brief bundles arguments and evidence. Statements presented inside a treatment—such as claims about a “long peace” or historical near-misses—are material to evaluate, not findings established by the experiment.</p>
</div>

## Takeaways {#takeaways}

<ol class="takeaway-list">
  <li><div>The security dilemma explains how defensive intentions can generate offensive-looking behavior and reciprocal insecurity.</div></li>
  <li><div>Deterrence requires more than possession: survivability, credibility, communication, and organizational control all matter.</div></li>
  <li><div>Sagan's three models identify distinct routes to acquisition; complex cases often contain evidence for more than one.</div></li>
  <li><div>The Waltz–Sagan debate separates the strategic logic of deterrence from the organizational and accidental risks of managing arsenals.</div></li>
  <li><div>Nonproliferation institutions change information and incentives, but they operate inside the same security environment that motivates acquisition.</div></li>
</ol>
