## Overview {#overview}

This lecture asks how nations are made, how nationalism becomes politically effective, and how the boundaries of belonging become racialized and gendered. It begins by separating nation, state, country, and ethnic group, then contrasts accounts that treat nations as ancient with modernist approaches centered on industrialization, mass education, and print capitalism.

Mainstream international relations often treats national identity as a fixed property of states. The lecture instead compares realist, liberal, constructivist, and Marxist readings of nationalism. Korea under Japanese colonial rule and Ukraine after Russia's invasions complicate the idea that civic nationalism is always inclusive and ethnic nationalism always exclusionary: institutional setting and political purpose matter. The second half turns to racialized membership and Joane Nagel's account of the masculinist nation, including patriotic motherhood, reproductive politics, and militarized sexuality.

### Questions guiding the lecture

<ol class="question-list">
  <li><div>How is a nation constructed rather than simply given?</div></li>
  <li><div>How do different IR traditions explain nationalism and national identity?</div></li>
  <li><div>How have race and ethnicity shaped the boundary between members and outsiders?</div></li>
  <li><div>How do gendered expectations organize national reproduction, protection, and sacrifice?</div></li>
  <li><div>When can nationalism sustain solidarity, and when does it become hierarchical or expansionist?</div></li>
</ol>

## Classroom study {#classroom-study}

<div class="study-panel">
  <p class="eyebrow">Descriptive classroom survey</p>
  <h3>What makes someone “truly” part of a nation?</h3>
  <p><strong>Question.</strong> Which membership criteria do participants consider important, how strongly do they express citizen preference, and how does the classroom pattern compare descriptively with selected ISSP 2023 benchmarks?</p>
  <p><strong>Design.</strong> This was not a randomized experiment. The survey included nationality and ideology items, citizen preference, and an eight-item battery covering birth, ancestry, citizenship, residence, language, religion, respect for institutions and laws, and felt membership.</p>
  <p><strong>Interpretive limit.</strong> The participating class is a convenience group, not a population sample. Cross-national benchmark comparisons are descriptive and some membership items do not have directly comparable ISSP measures.</p>
</div>

<p class="method-note">No response-level or aggregate student results are reproduced on this public site. The study is included to document what was asked and how the exercise connected definitions of nationhood to measurement.</p>

## Takeaways {#takeaways}

<ol class="takeaway-list">
  <li><div>Nations are institutional and symbolic accomplishments, not merely demographic facts.</div></li>
  <li><div>The civic–ethnic distinction is analytically useful, but context determines whether either form enables inclusion, exclusion, or collective survival.</div></li>
  <li><div>Race is produced through boundary-making and classification; it is not a detachable “addition” to national identity.</div></li>
  <li><div>National projects are gendered through roles of protection, sacrifice, reproduction, and representation.</div></li>
  <li><div>National solidarity can support redistribution and self-determination while also creating hierarchies of membership.</div></li>
</ol>
