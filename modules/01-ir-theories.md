## Overview {#overview}

The opening lecture in this sequence treats realism, liberalism, and constructivism as competing analytical lenses rather than a list of labels. Each tradition begins from different assumptions about the actors in world politics, the constraints they face, and the sources of continuity or change. Those choices determine which evidence matters and which outcomes require explanation.

Wendt's argument that anarchy has no single behavioral logic anchors the constructivist section. Mearsheimer's critique of the liberal international order then supplies a contemporary dispute on which all three theories can be compared. The goal is not to select a permanent winner. It is to build a disciplined comparison: what does each theory foreground, what does it leave out, and why can the same event support more than one defensible account?

### Questions guiding the lecture

<ol class="question-list">
  <li><div>What assumptions distinguish realism, liberalism, and constructivism?</div></li>
  <li><div>How does each tradition explain power, institutions, cooperation, and conflict?</div></li>
  <li><div>What follows if interests and identities are produced through interaction rather than fixed in advance?</div></li>
  <li><div>How well do the theories explain pressure on the liberal international order, Sino-U.S. competition, and Russia's war against Ukraine?</div></li>
</ol>

## Lecture map {#lecture-map}

<div class="lecture-map">
  <div><strong>Part 1</strong><p><b>Realism.</b> Anarchy, self-help, relative power, and the differences among classical, structural, offensive, defensive, and neoclassical variants.</p></div>
  <div><strong>Part 2</strong><p><b>Liberalism.</b> Interdependence, democratic constraints, international institutions, absolute gains, and the ambition of a liberal international order.</p></div>
  <div><strong>Part 3</strong><p><b>Constructivism.</b> Socially formed interests and identities, norms, and Wendt's argument that interaction gives anarchy its meaning.</p></div>
  <div><strong>Part 4</strong><p><b>Comparison in practice.</b> Contemporary cases are read through all three lenses to expose different mechanisms and blind spots.</p></div>
</div>

## Classroom study {#classroom-study}

<div class="study-panel">
  <p class="eyebrow">Randomized survey experiment</p>
  <h3>Framing U.S.–China cooperation</h3>
  <p><strong>Question.</strong> Does a rivalry-oriented, Hobbesian brief or a cooperation-oriented, Lockean brief change expectations about U.S.–China cooperation?</p>
  <p><strong>Design.</strong> Qualtrics assigned participants to one of the two analyst briefs or to an implemented control that received no brief. All participants then answered five 1–5 items combined into a Cooperation Perception Score after two items were reverse-coded.</p>
  <p><strong>Interpretive limit.</strong> The treatments introduced different claims and examples; the exercise tests the bundled briefs, not an isolated word or a universally generalizable “frame.”</p>
</div>

## Takeaways {#takeaways}

<ol class="takeaway-list">
  <li><div>Realism explains how anarchy and uncertainty make power and security persistent concerns, but its variants disagree about how much power states seek.</div></li>
  <li><div>Liberal theories identify mechanisms—trade, democracy, and institutions—that can alter incentives without eliminating conflict.</div></li>
  <li><div>Constructivism asks how interaction, norms, and identity produce the interests that other theories often take as given.</div></li>
  <li><div>Theories are selective tools. A strong analysis states what a framework explains, what evidence supports it, and what the framework misses.</div></li>
</ol>
