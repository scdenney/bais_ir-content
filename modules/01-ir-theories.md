## Overview {#overview}

The sequence opens by comparing realism, liberalism and constructivism as explanations of world politics. Each tradition makes different assumptions about political actors and the constraints they face. It also locates the sources of continuity and change in different places. Those choices determine which evidence matters and which outcomes require explanation.

Wendt's argument that anarchy has no single behavioral logic anchors the constructivist section. Mearsheimer's critique of the liberal international order then gives students a contemporary dispute through which to compare the theories. A disciplined comparison asks what each theory explains and which evidence supports it. It also identifies what the theory leaves out and why another account may remain defensible.

### Questions guiding the lecture

<ol class="question-list">
  <li><div>What assumptions distinguish realism, liberalism, and constructivism?</div></li>
  <li><div>How does each tradition explain power, institutions, cooperation, and conflict?</div></li>
  <li><div>What follows if interests and identities are produced through interaction rather than fixed in advance?</div></li>
  <li><div>How well do the theories explain pressure on the liberal international order, Sino-U.S. competition, and Russia's war against Ukraine?</div></li>
</ol>

## Lecture map {#lecture-map}

<div class="lecture-map">
  <div><strong>Part 1</strong><p>Realism starts from anarchy and self-help, then distinguishes relative power across its classical, structural and later variants.</p></div>
  <div><strong>Part 2</strong><p>Liberalism turns to interdependence and democratic constraints. It examines how institutions can enable absolute gains and sustain an international order.</p></div>
  <div><strong>Part 3</strong><p>Constructivism treats interests and identities as socially formed. Wendt's account shows how norms and interaction give anarchy its meaning.</p></div>
  <div><strong>Part 4</strong><p>Contemporary cases provide a common test. Reading each case through the three theories identifies their mechanisms and blind spots.</p></div>
</div>

## Classroom study {#classroom-study}

<div class="study-panel">
  <p class="eyebrow">Randomized survey experiment</p>
  <h3>Framing U.S.–China cooperation</h3>
  <p>The study asks whether a rivalry-oriented, Hobbesian brief or a cooperation-oriented, Lockean brief changes expectations about U.S.–China cooperation.</p>
  <p>Qualtrics assigned participants to one of the two analyst briefs or to a control that received no brief. All participants then answered five items on 1–5 scales. Two items were reverse-coded before the responses were combined into a Cooperation Perception Score.</p>
  <p>The treatments introduced different claims and examples, so the exercise tests each brief as a whole. It does not isolate a single word or estimate a framing effect that generalizes beyond the class.</p>

## Takeaways {#takeaways}

<ol class="takeaway-list">
  <li><div>Realism explains how anarchy and uncertainty make power and security persistent concerns, but its variants disagree about how much power states seek.</div></li>
  <li><div>Liberal theories show how trade and democratic constraints can alter incentives. Institutions may also support cooperation without eliminating conflict.</div></li>
  <li><div>Constructivism asks how interaction, norms, and identity produce the interests that other theories often take as given.</div></li>
  <li><div>Theories are selective tools. A strong analysis states what a framework explains, what evidence supports it, and what the framework misses.</div></li>
</ol>
